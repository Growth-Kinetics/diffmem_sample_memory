This file serves as the central entity for the sealed packet left by the late Dr. Lanyon for Mr. Utterson, containing his final testimony regarding Dr. Jekyll.

### 🔒 **Access & Sealing Conditions** [Strength: High]
*   **Outer Packet Superscription:** "PRIVATE: for the hands of G. J. Utterson ALONE, and in case of his predecease to be destroyed unread."
    ↳ **Implication:** Establishes a bond of absolute personal trust and secrecy between Lanyon and Utterson. The information is deemed too sensitive for anyone else.
*   **Inner Enclosure Inscription:** "not to be opened till the death or disappearance of Dr. Henry Jekyll."
    ↳ **Implication:** The contents directly concern Dr. Jekyll and are only to be revealed once he is no longer present. The term "disappearance" creates a direct and disturbing link to the terms of Jekyll's will.
*   **Custodian's Obligation (Fulfilled):** Utterson's sense of "professional honour" compelled him to keep the packet sealed. This obligation was fulfilled upon Jekyll's confirmed disappearance, at which point Jekyll's own final instructions directed Utterson to read Lanyon's narrative.
/END 🔒 **Access & Sealing Conditions**

### 📖 **Narrative Contents & Revelation** [Strength: High]
*   **Jekyll's Urgent Request:** On January 9th, Lanyon received a desperate letter from Jekyll. It instructed him to break into Jekyll's cabinet, retrieve a drawer (from press 'E') containing powders, a phial, and a book, and give it to a messenger who would arrive at midnight.
*   **The Midnight Messenger (Hyde):** At midnight, a small, repulsive man in oversized clothes arrived. Lanyon felt an "icy pang" and "disgustful curiosity" towards him. This man was Edward Hyde.
*   **The Metamorphosis:** In Lanyon's consulting room, Hyde mixed the drawer's contents—a white salt and a blood-red tincture—into a potion that changed from purple to green. After taunting Lanyon with the choice to witness a "prodigy," Hyde drank the mixture.
*   **The Prodigy:** Lanyon watched in abject terror as Hyde convulsed, swelled, and his features "seemed to melt and alter," transforming before his eyes into Dr. Henry Jekyll.
*   **The Confession & Aftermath:** Jekyll then confessed certain details to Lanyon, including that the creature who had entered his house was Hyde, the wanted murderer of Sir Danvers Carew. The shock of this event and the "moral turpitude" unveiled shattered Lanyon's worldview, destroyed his health, and led to his rapid death.
/END 📖 **Narrative Contents & Revelation**

### 🧠 **Context & Significance** [Strength: Medium]
*   **Origin Context:** Lanyon, on his deathbed following a great "shock," refused to see or speak of Jekyll, whom he regarded "as dead." He promised Utterson that "after I am dead, you may perhaps come to learn the right and wrong of this." This narrative is the promised explanation.
*   **Thematic Link to Jekyll's Will:** The condition of "disappearance" echoes the clause in Jekyll's will that would transfer his fortune to Hyde. Utterson is horrified to see this idea, which he associated with the "sinister suggestion of the man Hyde," now written "by the hand of Lanyon." This deepens the mystery and suggests Lanyon learned something that confirmed the will's strange provision.
*   **Psychological Impact on Utterson:** The narrative has resolved the central mystery from Lanyon's perspective, confirming Utterson's worst fears about the connection between Jekyll and Hyde. It replaces suspense with horror and incredulity. The narrative serves as the first of two final confessions (the other being Jekyll's) that fully explain the tragedy.
*   **Scientific & Moral Schism:** The narrative is the ultimate vindication of Jekyll's "transcendental medicine," which Lanyon had long derided as "unscientific balderdash." The revelation is not just a personal shock but a philosophical one, shattering Lanyon's materialistic worldview and leading to his death.
/END 🧠 **Context & Significance**
