This file serves as the central entity for Mr. Hyde, a mysterious and malevolent figure encountered in London. It captures his initial observed actions, physical impression, and enigmatic connections.

### 🌟 **Core Identity Mapping** [ALWAYS_LOAD] [Strength: High]
*   **[Name]** Mr. Hyde | **[Key Roles]** Antagonist, Murderer, Fugitive | **[Key Traits]** Detestable, malevolent, violent, sneering, capable of "ape-like fury" and "insensate cruelty."
            ↳ **Physicality:** A small, pale, and dwarfish man. He gives an impression of deformity without any nameable malformation. Observers describe him as "down-right detestable" and "hardly human," suggesting something "troglodytic."
            ↳ **Origin of Appearance (Jekyll's Confession):** Dr. Jekyll theorizes that Hyde is smaller, slighter, and younger because he is the embodiment of Jekyll's evil side, which had been "much less exercised and much less exhausted" than his virtuous side. Evil, being the "lethal side of man," left on Hyde's body an "imprint of deformity and decay." Initially, Hyde's body was smaller, but Jekyll noted that with repeated use, "the body of Edward Hyde had grown in stature."
        ↳ **Observed Details:** Dr. Lanyon notes a "shocking expression" and a "remarkable combination of great muscular activity and great apparent debility of constitution." His clothes, though of rich fabric, are "enormously too large for him." Found deceased by suicide (poison), with the body "sorely contorted and still twitching," wearing Dr. Jekyll's oversized clothes.
        ↳ **Emotional Signature:** Instantly inspires a deep-seated loathing, hatred, and fear in all observers.
            ↳ **Jekyll's Theory:** Jekyll posits that this universal revulsion occurs because all human beings are a "commingled out of good and evil," whereas "Edward Hyde, alone in the ranks of mankind, was pure evil."
        ↳ **Observer Experience:** Mr. Utterson experiences "disgust, loathing and fear." Dr. Lanyon describes the effect as an "odd, subjective disturbance" akin to "incipient rigour," accompanied by a "marked sinking of the pulse" and an "icy pang" upon physical contact. He attributes this not to simple hatred, but a cause lying "much deeper in the nature of man."
        ↳ **Communication Style:**
        *   **Spoken:** Speaks with a "husky, whispering and somewhat broken voice." His demeanor shifts rapidly. He can be coolly defiant ("That is my name. What do you want?"), civil ("I beg your pardon, Dr. Lanyon"), or frantically impatient ("Have you got it?"). He reacts to surprise with a "hissing intake of the breath" and anger with a "savage laugh." In his final hours, his voice is heard "complainingly" from behind the cabinet door, crying out "like a rat" when seen, begging for mercy ("Utterson, for God’s sake, have mercy!"), and emitting a "dismal screech, as of mere animal terror" when the door is broken down.
    *   **Written:** His supposed handwriting is an "odd, upright hand." A letter attributed to him reassures Jekyll of his safety and means of escape. However, this handwriting is discovered to be nearly identical to Dr. Jekyll's. In his final week, numerous notes in Jekyll's hand are passed under the door, desperately seeking a pure chemical.
    ↳ **Thinking Pattern:** Evolves significantly. Initially, he is an extension of Jekyll, embodying a "heady recklessness" and a "solution of the bonds of obligation," focused on "undignified" pleasures. Over time, he develops his own persona, centered on self, "drinking pleasure with bestial avidity from any degree of torture to another." After the Carew murder and becoming a fugitive, his mind sharpens, becoming "astute" and driven by the twin passions of "fear and hatred." He resents Jekyll's control and plays "ape-like tricks" (scrawling blasphemies, burning letters) out of spite. His terror of the gallows forces him to return to the Jekyll form, a "temporary suicide" he loathes.

### 💥 **Observed Incidents** [Strength: Medium]
*   **The Trampling Incident:**
    ↳ **Context:** Witnessed by Mr. Enfield around 3 a.m. on a winter morning in London. Jekyll refers to it as an "act of cruelty to a child."
    ↳ **Action:** Ran into a small girl (8-10 years old) at a corner and "trampled calmly over the child's body," leaving her screaming. The act was described as "hellish" and like a "damned Juggernaut."
    ↳ **Aftermath:** When apprehended, Hyde was "perfectly cool" and made no resistance. Faced with the threat of scandal, he agreed to pay £100. He retrieved the funds by providing a cheque signed in the name of Henry Jekyll. To prevent this link in the future, Jekyll opened a separate bank account for Hyde and created a unique signature for him by "sloping my own hand backward."
/END 💥 **Observed Incidents**

*   **Confrontation with Mr. Utterson:**
    ↳ **Context:** Mr. Utterson waited by the mysterious door at night to finally see the man named in Dr. Jekyll's will.
    ↳ **Action:** When approached, Hyde initially "shrank back with a hissing intake of the breath" but quickly recovered. He was defiant, demanding to know what Utterson wanted. He reluctantly showed his face.
    ↳ **Aftermath:** Hyde accused Utterson of lying about how he knew him, gave a "savage laugh," and quickly unlocked the door and vanished inside. He provided his address in Soho. Utterson was left deeply disturbed, describing Hyde as "pale and dwarfish" with a "murderous mixture of timidity and boldness."


*   **The Murder of Sir Danvers Carew:**
    ↳ **Context:** Witnessed by a maid servant. Occurred after Jekyll, in a moment of "moral weakness" following two months of abstinence, took the potion again. Jekyll states his "devil had been long caged, he came out roaring."
    ↳ **Action:** Provoked by Carew's civilities, Hyde experienced a "tempest of impatience." Jekyll describes the murder as an act of "transport of glee," where Hyde "mauled the unresisting body, tasting delight from every blow." He proceeded with "ape-like fury" until the "bones were audibly shattered."
    ↳ **Aftermath:** A "cold thrill of terror" struck Hyde mid-delirium. He fled to his Soho house, destroyed his papers, and had a song on his lips as he compounded and drank the potion to transform back, "pledg[ing] the dead man." The public disappearance was, in fact, Jekyll locking himself away, terrified of the gallows and resolving that "Hyde was thenceforth impossible."


*   **The Transformation at Lanyon's:**
    ↳ **Preceding Context (The Regent's Park Incident):** The event was precipitated by Jekyll spontaneously transforming into Hyde while sitting on a bench in Regent's Park. Now a "hunted, houseless, a known murderer," Hyde could not enter Jekyll's house. His faculties "sharpened," he devised a plan to get help. He took a cab to a hotel, where his fury terrified the staff, and wrote two letters: one to Lanyon (to retrieve the drugs) and one to Poole.
    ↳ **At Lanyon's House:** Summoned by the desperate letter, Dr. Lanyon retrieves the chemicals. At midnight, he is visited by Hyde, who is described as a "creature new to me; shaken with inordinate anger, strung to the pitch of murder, lusting to inflict pain. Yet the creature was astute."
    ↳ **Action & Aftermath:** Hyde mixes and drinks the potion, transforming back into Jekyll in front of the horrified Lanyon. The shock of this "prodigy" and Jekyll's confession of "moral turpitude" causes Lanyon's health to collapse, leading to his death.


*   **The Last Night & Suicide:**
    ↳ **Context:** The crisis was caused by Jekyll's original supply of chemical salt running out. He discovered that the original batch contained an "unknown impurity" which was the active ingredient, and all new, pure supplies were ineffective. He was permanently trapped as Hyde.
    ↳ **Behavior:** The person in the cabinet (Hyde) has been pacing relentlessly and weeping. Jekyll's confession, written with the last of the old powder, predicts this state: "I know how I shall sit shuddering and weeping in my chair, or continue, with the most strained and fearstruck ecstasy of listening, to pace up and down this room." The desperate search for the chemical was a futile attempt to find more of the impure salt.
    ↳ **Action:** When Utterson demands entry, Hyde's voice cries out for mercy. Utterson and Poole break down the door with an axe.
    ↳ **Aftermath:** They find the body of Edward Hyde, who has died by suicide via poison. This aligns with Jekyll's final speculation: "Will Hyde die upon the scaffold? or will he find courage to release himself at the last moment? God knows; I am careless."

### 🔑 **Mysterious Connections** [Strength: High]
*   **The Lanyon Revelation (The Transformation):** The ultimate connection is revealed in Dr. Lanyon's posthumous narrative. Lanyon witnesses Mr. Hyde, in a state of extreme distress, consume a chemical potion prepared from ingredients retrieved from Jekyll's lab. Hyde then undergoes a violent, horrifying physical transformation, reverting back into the form of Dr. Henry Jekyll. Jekyll confesses to Lanyon that the creature was indeed Hyde, the hunted murderer of Sir Danvers Carew. This event is the direct cause of Lanyon's fatal decline.
*   **Dr. Jekyll's Will:** Mr. Hyde is the sole beneficiary of Dr. Henry Jekyll's will, set to inherit a quarter of a million sterling. In the event of Jekyll's death or "disappearance or unexplained absence for any period exceeding three calendar months," Hyde is to "step into the said Henry Jekyll's shoes."
*   **The Murder Weapon:** The heavy cane used to murder Sir Danvers Carew is recognized by Utterson as one he himself had gifted to Dr. Jekyll years prior, directly linking Jekyll's property to Hyde's crime. The other half of the broken stick was found behind the door in Hyde's Soho rooms.
*   **Soho Lodgings:** Hyde's rooms in a "dismal" Soho district are found to be furnished with "luxury and good taste," including silver, fine napery, and a good picture, presumed to be gifts from Jekyll. The rooms were found ransacked, with ashes in the fireplace from burned papers and the butt end of a green cheque book.
*   **Financial Link:** A bank account in Hyde's name contains "several thousand pounds." The discovery of a burned cheque book suggests a frantic attempt to destroy evidence.
*   **The Laboratory Door:** The "sinister block of building" Hyde has a key to is the old dissecting room, which is part of Dr. Jekyll's property.
*   **The "Hyde" Letter & Forgery:** After the Carew murder, Jekyll provides Utterson with a letter, supposedly from Hyde.
    ↳ **Content:** Written in an "odd, upright hand," the letter claims Hyde has a secure "means of escape" and that Jekyll should not fear for his safety.
    ↳ **Delivery:** Jekyll claims the letter was handed in without a postmarked envelope (which he burned), but his butler, Poole, denies any messengers arrived, suggesting the letter originated from within the laboratory.
    ↳ **Handwriting Analysis:** Utterson's clerk, Mr. Guest, compares the letter to Jekyll's own writing and finds a "singular resemblance," concluding the two hands are "in many points identical: only differently sloped." This leads Utterson to believe Jekyll forged the letter for the murderer.
*   **Jekyll's Fluctuating State:** After the murder, Jekyll swears to Utterson he is "done with" Hyde. Following Hyde's public disappearance, Jekyll enters a period of renewal for over two months. This recovery abruptly ends, and he falls into a state of "extreme seclusion." This is witnessed directly by Utterson and Enfield, who see Jekyll at his window looking like a "disconsolate prisoner." As they speak, Jekyll's face is suddenly overcome with an expression of "abject terror and despair" before the window is slammed shut, leaving the onlookers horrified. This event precedes a letter where Jekyll speaks of a self-inflicted "punishment and a danger that I cannot name," referring to himself as the "chief of sinners" and "chief of sufferers."
*   **Hyde's Influence on the Will:** Jekyll confirms to Utterson that it was Hyde who dictated the terms of his will, particularly the clause regarding his "disappearance."
*   **Utterson's Promise:** Jekyll previously extracted a promise from Utterson to "get his rights for him" should Jekyll be "taken away." This promise now haunts Utterson in light of the murder and Jekyll's suspicious behavior.

*   **The Final Will & Confession:** Found in the cabinet after Hyde's death is a new will, dated that day, which replaces Hyde's name with Gabriel John Utterson's. Also found is a note from Jekyll instructing Utterson to read Dr. Lanyon's narrative and then a "considerable packet" containing Jekyll's full confession.
*   **Blasphemous Annotations:** A pious book, highly esteemed by Jekyll, is found filled with "startling blasphemies." Jekyll's confession reveals these were not his own sentiments but "ape-like tricks" played by Hyde, who, out of hatred for Jekyll, would scrawl blasphemies in Jekyll's books and destroy his letters and a portrait of his father.
/END 🔑 **Mysterious Connections**

This resentment manifested in "ape-like tricks" like defacing Jekyll's books.
        *   **Final State:** In the end, Jekyll is horrified by Hyde, while Hyde is driven by a mix of terror (of the gallows) and hatred for Jekyll. Jekyll notes Hyde's strange "attachment" is born from the fear that Jekyll could end both their lives via suicide, a power that makes Jekyll "pity him."

He is convinced Dr. Jekyll has been murdered and that the "dwarfish" figure he glimpsed in the lab is the killer, whom he identifies as Hyde. His testimony about the changed voice, the figure's small stature, and the desperate need for a specific drug convinces Utterson to act. He bravely assists Utterson in breaking down the cabinet door with an axe.

Summoned by a desperate letter from Jekyll, he retrieves a drawer of chemicals and is visited at midnight by Hyde. Lanyon is filled with "disgustful curiosity" and horror at his visitor. After Hyde transforms into Jekyll before his eyes, Lanyon's "life is shaken to its roots." The "moral turpitude" Jekyll confesses to him, combined with the shock of the transformation, leads to his rapid death. His posthumous letter to Utterson provides the definitive account of the Hyde-Jekyll identity.

### 🧭 **Moral & Ethical Profile** [Strength: High]
*   **Core Philosophy:** Hyde is the literal embodiment of "pure evil," separated from the balancing influence of good that exists in other humans. He was created by Jekyll to act as a vehicle for pleasure without consequence, a being with a "solution of the bonds of obligation." His philosophy is one of total self-centeredness, "drinking pleasure with bestial avidity from any degree of torture to another."
*   **Guiding Principles:** His actions are guided by impulse and a "furious propensity to ill." Initially, this is directed toward "undignified" pleasures, but it escalates to monstrous cruelty (the Carew murder), which he commits with a "transport of glee." After becoming a wanted man, his guiding principles become fear and hatred: fear of the gallows and hatred for Jekyll. He is "relentless like a man of stone" but also capable of astute, cunning planning when his survival is at stake.
*   **Internal Conflict:** The conflict is not within Hyde, but between Hyde and Jekyll. Hyde himself is "pure evil" and feels no remorse, only a "cold thrill of terror" when his own life is endangered. His internal state is a "tempest" of base passions. His weeping in the final days is not from guilt, but from the terror of his predicament—hunted, and trapped in a form he cannot escape due to the failure of the potion. His final act of suicide is a choice between the gallows and self-destruction, an act of a cornered animal, not of penitence.
