### 🔗 **Relationship Dynamics** [Strength: Medium]
*   **General Pattern:** Tends to be the "last reputable acquaintance and the last good influence" in the lives of "downgoing men." He offers unwavering, non-judgmental support.
*   **Friendship Formation:** Accepts his friendly circle "ready-made from the hands of opportunity." His affections are a "growth of time," like ivy, implying no specific "aptness in the object."
*   **Known Associates:**
        ↳ **Dr. Henry Jekyll:** One of Utterson's oldest friends. After a brief social reprieve, Jekyll's complete self-isolation prompts his terrified butler, Poole, to seek Utterson's help, fearing foul play. Utterson, now believing Jekyll has been murdered by Hyde, leads the charge to break into the cabinet. He finds no trace of Jekyll, but discovers a new will leaving the entire estate to Utterson himself, along with a final letter from Jekyll directing him to read Lanyon's narrative and Jekyll's own enclosed confession. The mystery of his friend's fate deepens, even as Utterson holds the explanation in his hands.
    ↳ **Dr. Lanyon:** An old friend of both Utterson and Jekyll. After a long estrangement over Jekyll's "scientific heresies," they briefly reconcile at a dinner party. Shortly after, Utterson finds Lanyon mortally ill, having suffered a "shock" from which he cannot recover. Lanyon vehemently severs all ties with Jekyll, whom he now regards "as dead." He dies soon after, leaving Utterson a sealed letter not to be opened until Jekyll's death or disappearance, deepening the mystery.
    ↳ **Mr. Richard Enfield:** A distant kinsman and "well-known man about town." Their bond is characterized by silent Sunday walks. During one such walk, it is revealed Enfield now knows the by-street door is a back entrance to Jekyll's. They witness the "Incident at the Window" together, a shared moment of horror that freezes their blood and leaves them walking away in shocked silence.
        ↳ **Mr. Edward Hyde:** The beneficiary of Jekyll's original will. Utterson's relationship with Hyde is defined by "disgust, loathing and fear." After confirming Hyde murdered Sir Danvers Carew, Utterson's fears escalate until he believes Hyde has also murdered Jekyll. This belief drives him to break into the laboratory, where he discovers Hyde's body, dead by suicide, dressed in Jekyll's oversized clothes.
        ↳ **Poole:** Dr. Jekyll's loyal and long-serving butler. His growing terror over his master's seclusion and the strange events in the cabinet culminates in a desperate visit to Utterson. Convinced Jekyll has been murdered and an imposter is in his place, Poole's conviction and fear finally push Utterson to abandon his policy of non-interference. He accompanies Utterson, axe in hand, to break down the cabinet door, becoming a key partner in the final discovery.

    ↳ **Sir Danvers Carew:** A prominent and respected gentleman, M.P., and a client of Utterson's. Murdered by Mr. Hyde. An envelope addressed to Utterson was found on his body, leading the police to the lawyer.
    ↳ **Inspector Newcomen:** An officer from Scotland Yard. Utterson assists him in the investigation of the Carew murder, leading him to Hyde's lodgings in Soho.
    ↳ **Mr. Guest:** Utterson's head clerk and a trusted confidant. Guest is a "great student and critic of handwriting," and Utterson relies on his expertise and counsel when faced with the mysterious letter from Hyde.

### 📓 **Noteworthy Events & Observations** [Strength: High]
*   **The Story of the Door:** Became privy to Mr. Enfield's story about a sinister door and a detestable man named Hyde. The story deeply troubles him as he knows the identity of the man who signed the cheque Hyde used: his friend, Dr. Henry Jekyll.
*   **The Matter of the Will:** Disturbed by Enfield's story, Utterson retrieves and re-reads Dr. Jekyll's will, which he holds in his safe. The will dictates that all of Jekyll's possessions should pass to Edward Hyde in the event of Jekyll's death or "disappearance or unexplained absence" for more than three months. This document has long been his "eyesore."
*   **Consultation with Lanyon:** Seeking information, Utterson visits their mutual friend, Dr. Lanyon. He learns that Lanyon has not seen Jekyll in over a decade due to a scientific dispute, and Lanyon has never heard of Mr. Hyde.
*   **The Hunt for Mr. Hyde:** Utterson becomes consumed by a "singularly strong, almost an inordinate, curiosity" to see Hyde's face. He suffers from nightmares and begins to "haunt the door" at all hours, determined to catch a glimpse of the man.
*   **Encounter with Hyde:** Utterson confronts Hyde as he is entering the by-street door. Hyde is described as "pale and dwarfish," giving an impression of deformity. The encounter fills Utterson with "disgust, loathing and fear." Hyde provides his address in Soho before quickly disappearing into the house. Utterson concludes he has seen "Satan's signature upon a face."
*   **Confirmation from Poole:** Utterson proceeds to Jekyll's main residence and speaks with the butler, Poole. Poole confirms that Hyde has a key, comes and goes via the laboratory (the by-street door), and that the entire staff has orders to obey him.
*   **Formulation of a Theory:** Utterson concludes that Jekyll is being blackmailed for a past sin and that Hyde, aware of the will, poses a mortal danger to his friend. This solidifies his resolve to intervene and help Jekyll.

*   **The Dinner with Jekyll:** After a dinner party, Utterson confronts Jekyll about the will and Mr. Hyde. Jekyll becomes visibly distressed, refuses to confide in Utterson, and dismisses the lawyer's fears by claiming he can be "rid of Mr. Hyde" at any moment. He then makes Utterson promise to ensure justice for Hyde and protect his rights if Jekyll were to disappear, leaving Utterson to reluctantly agree.


*   **The Carew Murder Case:** Nearly a year later, Utterson is summoned by the police when a client, Sir Danvers Carew, is brutally murdered. An envelope with Utterson's name on it is found on the body.
    ↳ **Identification of Victim & Weapon:** At the police station, Utterson identifies the victim. He is then shown the murder weapon, a broken half of a heavy cane, which he recognizes with horror as one he had gifted to Henry Jekyll years prior.
    ↳ **Leading the Investigation:** After the police describe the suspect as a small man, Utterson's suspicion of Hyde is confirmed. He leads Inspector Newcomen to Hyde's address in a gloomy, nightmarish district of Soho.
    ↳ **Search of Hyde's Lodgings:** They find Hyde's rooms have been ransacked. Evidence is discovered, including the other half of the cane and the burnt remains of a cheque book. The inspector believes Hyde has incriminated himself and can be caught at the bank.
    ↳ **The Fugitive's Identity:** A major obstacle to the investigation arises: Hyde has no known family, has never been photographed, and descriptions of him from the few who have seen him are inconsistent, agreeing only on a "haunting sense of unexpressed deformity."

*   **Visit to a "Deathly Sick" Jekyll:** Following the murder, Utterson visits Jekyll in his cabinet, a room he'd never been in before. He finds Jekyll looking "deathly sick" and receives a feverish promise that Jekyll is "done with" Hyde forever.
*   **The Hyde Letter:** Jekyll gives Utterson a letter, supposedly from Hyde, stating he has a safe means of escape. Jekyll claims to have burned the envelope, which was hand-delivered without a postmark. Utterson is initially relieved, believing it clears Jekyll of a worse intimacy.
*   **Poole's Contradiction:** On leaving, Utterson questions Poole, who is positive no letter was hand-delivered that day. This renews Utterson's fears, making him suspect the letter was written within the cabinet itself.
*   **Consultation with Mr. Guest:** Feeling a "longing for advice," Utterson consults his head clerk, Mr. Guest, an expert in handwriting. He shows Guest the letter from "Hyde."
*   **The Horrifying Discovery:** By chance, a dinner invitation from Jekyll arrives. Guest compares the two documents and discovers the handwriting is "in many points identical: only differently sloped."
*   **Utterson's Conclusion:** Utterson is horrified by the implication. He locks the letter in his safe and concludes, "Henry Jekyll forge for a murderer!" His blood runs cold.

*   **A Period of Calm:** Following the murder, Mr. Hyde vanishes. For two months, Utterson is relieved as Dr. Jekyll emerges from his seclusion, becoming more social and charitable than ever. Utterson, Jekyll, and Lanyon even dine together, seemingly reunited as inseparable friends.
*   **Jekyll's Sudden Relapse:** The calm is shattered when Jekyll abruptly refuses to see Utterson, confining himself to his house. This sudden return to solitude weighs heavily on the lawyer's spirits.
*   **The Shock of Dr. Lanyon:** Utterson visits Lanyon and is shocked to find him on his deathbed, pale and terrified. Lanyon speaks of having had a "shock" and declares he is "quite done with" Dr. Jekyll, whom he wishes to hear of no more.
*   **Jekyll's Cryptic Letter:** In response to a letter from Utterson, Jekyll writes back confirming the break with Lanyon is permanent and that he intends to live in "extreme seclusion" due to a "punishment and a danger" he has brought upon himself. He begs Utterson to respect his silence.
*   **The Death of Lanyon and the Mysterious Packet:** Lanyon dies shortly after their meeting. After the funeral, Utterson opens a letter Lanyon left for him. Inside is another sealed envelope, marked "not to be opened till the death or disappearance of Dr. Henry Jekyll." The reappearance of the word "disappearance" from Jekyll's will deeply disturbs Utterson, but he honors his friend's instructions and locks the packet away.
*   **Waning Friendship:** Utterson's desire to see Jekyll fades, replaced by fear and disquiet. He continues to call but is relieved to be turned away, preferring to get brief, unchanging reports from Poole that the doctor remains confined to his cabinet. His visits become less and less frequent.

*   **The Incident at the Window:** During a Sunday walk with Enfield, Utterson sees Jekyll at his cabinet window, looking like a "disconsolate prisoner." They exchange a few words before an expression of "abject terror and despair" strikes Jekyll's face, and the window is slammed shut. The event leaves both Utterson and Enfield pale and horrified, with Utterson muttering, "God forgive us." This encounter solidifies the terror surrounding Jekyll's condition.


*   **The Last Night - Poole's Appeal:** Utterson's quiet evening is shattered by a visit from a terrified Poole, who is convinced Dr. Jekyll has been murdered and an imposter is hiding in the cabinet. Poole describes a changed voice, desperate notes demanding a specific chemical, and a fleeting glimpse of a small, masked figure.
*   **The Last Night - A Theory of Disease:** Utterson initially theorizes that Jekyll is suffering from a transformative disease that has altered his appearance and voice. However, Poole's insistence that the figure was a "dwarf" and not his tall master, and his belief that the figure is Mr. Hyde, finally convinces Utterson.
*   **The Last Night - Vengeance and the Axe:** Utterson's fear for his friend solidifies into a new resolve. Believing Jekyll is dead and his murderer is in the cabinet, he declares, "let our name be vengeance." He and Poole arm themselves with a poker and an axe and proceed to the laboratory.
*   **The Last Night - Breaking Down the Door:** After a voice from within—which Utterson identifies as Hyde's—begs for mercy, Utterson gives the order: "Down with the door, Poole!" They smash their way into the cabinet.
*   **The Last Night - The Discovery:** Inside the quiet, orderly room, they find the body of Edward Hyde, dressed in Jekyll's oversized clothes, dead by suicide. There is no sign of Dr. Jekyll.
*   **The Last Night - The Final Documents:** On Jekyll's business table, Utterson discovers three crucial items: 1) A new will, replacing Hyde's name with his own, Gabriel John Utterson. 2) A brief note from Jekyll, dated that day, instructing him to read Lanyon's narrative first. 3) A bulky, sealed confession from Jekyll. Utterson takes the documents home to read, intending to save his friend's reputation before involving the police.
