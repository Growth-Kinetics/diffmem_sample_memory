### 🔗 **Relationship Dynamics** [Strength: High]
*   **Mr. Edward Hyde:** [Status: Murderer, Fugitive] Designated as Jekyll's heir. After murdering Sir Danvers Carew, Hyde is a fugitive. In the aftermath, Jekyll swears to Utterson he is "done with him in this world." Jekyll produces a letter, allegedly from Hyde, stating Hyde has a safe means of escape. Jekyll claims the letter was handed in, but this is contradicted by his butler, Poole. Upon inspection by Mr. Guest, a handwriting expert, the letter's script is found to be "in many points identical" to Jekyll's own, merely sloped differently. This leads Utterson to the horrifying conclusion that "Henry Jekyll forge for a murderer!"
*   **Mr. Gabriel John Utterson:** An old friend and his lawyer. After the Carew murder, Jekyll entrusts Utterson with a letter supposedly from Hyde. Disturbed by Jekyll's manner and a contradiction from the butler Poole, Utterson consults his head clerk, Mr. Guest, a handwriting expert. Guest determines that Jekyll's and Hyde's handwriting are nearly identical. This discovery leads Utterson to the terrible suspicion that Jekyll forged the letter to protect a murderer. He also learns from Jekyll that Hyde dictated the disappearance clause in the will, confirming his fear that Hyde intended to murder his friend.
*   **Dr. Hastie Lanyon:** An old friend from school and college, now estranged. Lanyon considers Jekyll's work "unscientific balderdash." Jekyll reciprocates the sentiment, calling Lanyon a "hide-bound pedant," an "ignorant, blatant pedant," and expresses he was "never more disappointed in any man than Lanyon" due to his friend's dismissal of his "scientific heresies."
*   **Poole:** Jekyll's loyal, elderly butler. He confirms that the household staff have orders to obey Mr. Hyde. His testimony is critical in raising Utterson's suspicions, as he states that no letters were delivered by hand on the day Jekyll claimed to have received one from Hyde, contradicting his master's story.
*   **Mr. Guest:** Mr. Utterson's head clerk and a "great student and critic of handwriting." After being shown the letter from "Hyde" and a dinner invitation from Jekyll, Guest observes a "singular resemblance" between the two, noting they are "in many points identical: only differently sloped." His expert opinion is the catalyst for Utterson's realization that Jekyll may have forged the letter.
