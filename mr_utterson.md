This walk culminates in the "Incident at the Window," where both men witness Jekyll's horrifying transformation of expression. They flee in shared, silent terror, a traumatic event that deepens their bond beyond quiet companionship.
    ↳ **Dr. Henry Jekyll:** [Status: Disappeared/Deceased] Jekyll's final crisis prompts a terrified Poole to summon Utterson. Convinced Jekyll has been murdered by a small, masked figure (whom they both suspect is Hyde) locked in the cabinet, they break down the door. They find Hyde's body, but Jekyll has vanished. Utterson discovers three crucial documents left for him: a new will leaving Jekyll's entire fortune to Utterson himself; a brief note from Jekyll dated that very day, confirming his imminent "disappearance"; and a bulky confession. The note instructs Utterson to first read Lanyon's narrative, finally providing the key to unlock the first part of the mystery. Jekyll's final act is to orchestrate the revelation of his secret through these documents, leaving Utterson to piece together the truth and manage the aftermath.
    ↳ **Mr. Hyde:** [Status: Deceased (Suicide)] After a week of terrorizing Jekyll's household from within the locked cabinet, Hyde is found dead by Utterson and Poole. Dressed in Jekyll's oversized clothes and having taken poison, his death is ruled a suicide. Utterson, initially believing Hyde murdered Jekyll and was hiding in the room, recognizes the body and the smell of poison. The discovery of Hyde's body inside Jekyll's private cabinet marks the climax of Utterson's investigation, leading directly to the discovery of Jekyll's final letters.
    ↳ **Dr. Hastie Lanyon:** [Status: Deceased] Utterson finds his old friend shockingly changed, with his "death-warrant written legibly upon his face" and suffering from a "deep-seated terror of the mind." Lanyon reveals he has had a "shock" from which he will never recover and is a "doomed man." He violently renounces his friendship with Jekyll, declaring "I am quite done with that person" and begging Utterson to spare him any mention of the man. He dies less than a fortnight later, leaving Utterson a sealed letter containing his narrative. The condition for opening it—the death or disappearance of Jekyll—is met on the night Utterson breaks into the cabinet, and Jekyll's own final note explicitly instructs Utterson to read it.
    ↳ **Poole:** Dr. Jekyll's loyal butler of twenty years. His terror becomes the catalyst for the final confrontation. After a week of strange behavior from the cabinet—a changed voice, desperate notes for a specific chemical, and a fleeting glimpse of a small, masked figure he believes is not his master—Poole goes to Utterson, convinced of "foul play." His detailed, fearful testimony persuades Utterson to take action. He accompanies Utterson back to the house, helps manage the terrified staff, and provides the axe to break down the cabinet door. His conviction that Jekyll was murdered and that the smaller figure was Hyde provides Utterson with the framework for his final actions.
    ↳ **Sir Danvers Carew:** [Status: Deceased | Client] An aged, white-haired, and well-regarded gentleman—and a client of Utterson's—murdered by Mr. Hyde. Utterson identified his body at the police station. An envelope addressed to Utterson was found on his person, which led the police to involve the lawyer in the case. The murder of a client adds a layer of professional duty to Utterson's personal horror.
    ↳ **Inspector Newcomen:** [Status: Investigating] An officer from Scotland Yard investigating the murder of Sir Danvers Carew. Utterson assists him by identifying the victim, recognizing the murder weapon as belonging to Jekyll, and leading him to Hyde's lodgings in Soho.

    ↳ **Mr. Guest:** Utterson's head clerk and a trusted confidant. Guest is a "great student and critic of handwriting." Utterson shows him the letter supposedly from Hyde. By chance, Guest is also able to see a dinner invitation from Jekyll and compares the two scripts. He concludes that the two hands are "in many points identical: only differently sloped," providing the crucial evidence that leads Utterson to believe Jekyll forged the letter.

*   **Key Professional Conflict:** The "lawyer's eyesore"—Jekyll's original will leaving everything to Hyde—is unexpectedly superseded. In the cabinet, beside Hyde's body, Utterson finds a new will, written in Jekyll's hand, with identical eccentric terms but with a shocking change: the sole beneficiary is now "Gabriel John Utterson." This discovery, made in the presence of the dead Hyde, leaves Utterson reeling with amazement and confusion, as Hyde would have had every reason to destroy it. This places Utterson in the legally and ethically complex position of being the executor and primary heir to a fortune at the center of a horrific mystery.
