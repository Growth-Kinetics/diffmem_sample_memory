This resolves the mystery of Jekyll's "disappearance" and re-contextualizes the scene in the cabinet: the man Poole saw was Jekyll in his Hyde form, trapped and desperate for the chemicals Lanyon was tasked to retrieve. Jekyll's final act was to leave behind the documents—Lanyon's narrative and his own confession—that would force Utterson to confront the unbelievable reality of his dual existence and subsequent death as Hyde.
    ↳ **Mr. Hyde:** [Status: Deceased (Identity Revealed)] Utterson now knows from Lanyon's account that Hyde was not a separate individual but a physical transformation of Dr. Jekyll. Lanyon's letter describes Hyde as a small, revolting creature who, upon drinking a potion, turned back into Jekyll before his eyes. This confirms that the body Utterson found in the cabinet was Jekyll's, albeit in the form of Hyde. The suicide was Jekyll's final, desperate act, trapped in a body that was not his own and hunted as the murderer of Sir Danvers Carew. The oversized clothes are now understood to be Jekyll's own, fitting his alternate form poorly.
    ↳ **Dr. Hastie Lanyon:** [Status: Deceased] Having now read Lanyon's narrative, Utterson understands the precise nature of the "shock" that killed his friend. Lanyon's letter recounts in horrifying detail how he followed Jekyll's desperate instructions, retrieved a drawer of chemicals from Jekyll's cabinet, and gave them to a messenger who was revealed to be Hyde. Lanyon then witnessed Hyde drink the potion and physically transform back into Henry Jekyll. This prodigy shattered Lanyon's scientific worldview and his very soul, leading to his rapid decline and death. His renunciation of Jekyll is now understood as a reaction to this unbearable truth. Lanyon's testimony provides Utterson with the first irrefutable, eyewitness proof of Jekyll's terrible secret.
    ↳ **Poole:** Dr. Jekyll's loyal butler of twenty years. His terror becomes the catalyst for the final confrontation. After a week of strange behavior from the cabinet—a changed voice, desperate notes for a specific chemical, and a fleeting glimpse of a small, masked figure he believes is not his master—Poole goes to Utterson, convinced of "foul play." His detailed, fearful testimony persuades Utterson to take action. He accompanies Utterson back to the house, helps manage the terrified staff, and provides the axe to break down the cabinet door. His conviction that Jekyll was murdered and that the smaller figure was Hyde provides Utterson with the framework for his final actions.
    ↳ **Sir Danvers Carew:** [Status: Deceased | Client] An aged, white-haired, and well-regarded gentleman—and a client of Utterson's—murdered by Mr. Hyde. Utterson identified his body at the police station. An envelope addressed to Utterson was found on his person, which led the police to involve the lawyer in the case. The murder of a client adds a layer of professional duty to Utterson's personal horror.
    ↳ **Inspector Newcomen:** [Status: Investigating] An officer from Scotland Yard investigating the murder of Sir Danvers Carew. Utterson assists him by identifying the victim, recognizing the murder weapon as belonging to Jekyll, and leading him to Hyde's lodgings in Soho.

    ↳ **Mr. Guest:** Utterson's head clerk and a trusted confidant. Guest is a "great student and critic of handwriting." Utterson shows him the letter supposedly from Hyde. By chance, Guest is also able to see a dinner invitation from Jekyll and compares the two scripts. He concludes that the two hands are "in many points identical: only differently sloped," providing the crucial evidence that leads Utterson to believe Jekyll forged the letter.

This discovery, made in the presence of the dead Hyde, leaves Utterson reeling with amazement and confusion, as Hyde would have had every reason to destroy it. This places Utterson in the legally and ethically complex position of being the executor and primary heir to a fortune at the center of a horrific mystery.
